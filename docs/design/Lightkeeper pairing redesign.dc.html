This file is the iteration history — turns 1 to 4, all the directions and the seven day-editor options. The settled work lives in Lightkeeper pairing flows: 4g implemented, and the paradigm rolled out to all five devices, one row each.

Which I would ship

4g is the one to build — 4f's model on 3a's round handles, with each zone's time folded into that zone's card so nothing floats loose between the strip and the controls. Say the word and I will wire it up in 3a: draggable handles, live gradient, offsets recomputed against today's sun.

4f is the same model in zone tiles. Sun-relative boundaries are the correct model — a fixed 21:00 is wrong twice a year — and drawing sunrise and sunset on the strip makes the offset something you can see rather than compute. Three independent temperatures is the real cost: one more decision at setup, and a no-sunrise fallback that settings has to own.

4e, if two decisions beat three. It supersedes 4b: same zones, named for the time of day rather than the colour, with both boundary times on the screen — draggable grips on the strip and stepper fields under it. The one thing to decide is whether morning and evening stay a single setting; the layout supports splitting them without a redesign.

4b, for reference. Selecting “warm” lighting up both ends of the day teaches the model in the gesture rather than in a label, it keeps one tappable day on screen, and it is the only option that removes the misleading affordance instead of annotating it. 4c is the most truthful picture and the right thing for the curve driver, where the shape is the point — but here it turns a two-decision screen into a chart. 4a is the cheapest patch if the current build is close to done. Say which and I will wire it into 3a and use the same idiom for curve and daylight.

Turn 3

Circadian, every screen

The whole driver in 2a's rules — one control per screen, one sentence at most, defaults already chosen. 3a is the path a user walks; 3b is the side trips and the states that decide whether the path survives contact. Once this reads right, the same skeleton stretches over curve, daylight, schedule and controller.

3a

The happy path: an intro and three steps

The intro says what the device does and names the three decisions; the three steps make them. A user who trusts the defaults taps Start, ticks a room and presses Next twice. Homey's own screen asks for the name afterwards, so pairing never does.

Circadian flow Intro→ Lights→ Your day→ Review Intro is unnumbered — the three steps it promises are the three that carry a step dot. Brightness and Try it are pushed from Your day and come back to it.

Open questions before this stretches to the other four

Does the intro screen survive contact with a returning user? Second and third time round it is a tap in the way. The cheap fix is to remember that the driver has been paired once and open on step 1, with the intro reachable from a row — but that hides the one screen that teaches the model, so I would rather ask than assume.

Is Review worth a third screen now that naming is Homey's job? It is confirmation and the only exit from a one-way door — real value, but thinner than it was. If four screens is too many, it merges into Your day as a readback above Next and the flow becomes Intro → Lights → Your day.

Turn 2

Strip it to one decision per screen

You are right that 1a and 1b still do three jobs at once: set the thing, teach the model, and warn about the edges. 2a does only the first. Every explanation either becomes the control's own behaviour or moves out of pairing entirely — to the device's settings page, where it is read when it matters rather than when the user is trying to finish.

2a

One decision per screen

Three rules, applied without exception: a screen holds one control and at most one sentence; anything secondary is a single row with a chevron; every caveat leaves pairing. Defaults are chosen for the user, so a screen can be finished by pressing Next.

Where the words went

Roughly 70% of the copy left these screens, and none of it was deleted as content. Three destinations: the device's settings page takes every caveat that is true after setup — missed offs, one lamp per device, stale sensors, what a ramp does; the review screen takes the readback; and the controls themselves take the rest, by being obvious enough not to need a paragraph.

The cost is honest: a first-timer is told less, and two screens become three. If a caveat turns out to be load-bearing at the moment of choosing, the rule to break is “one sentence” — not “one control”.

Turn 1

The five screens people get stuck on, twice over

Same five drivers, same pairing container, same tokens and 390px width. Two directions that differ on one axis: 1a leads with recognisable presets and hides the machinery until you ask; 1b makes the setting directly manipulable — you drag the thing you are describing. Pick one wholesale, or mix per driver.

What the current screens ask of the user
Two abstract sliders
Warmest and coolest are set with no idea what the day between them looks like, and the only feedback is a label reading “Warmest”.
A dropdown plus an orphan
“Follow the daylight” leaves a brightness slider below it that still moves, still shows 55%, and no longer means what it says. Three sentences of help text carry the difference.
Two lux numbers
Dark at 5, bright at 500 are the app's two hardest questions, and the user's only instrument is the readings list above them. Your own measurements say 500 lx is a kitchen number and wrong for an interior room.
A function-first grid
Seven lighting functions × a dropdown of remote events, nested in per-light accordions. The user holds the remote and thinks “what should this button do” — the screen asks the question backwards, and “Not assigned” × 5 reads as unfinished work.
An API key, first
The chore arrives before any value is visible. It only gates Flow writes at save — so both directions move it behind the work, where the user already wants the outcome.
1a

Presets first, machinery on request

Every abstract control is fronted by two or three named outcomes a person can recognise without learning the model. The sliders, the lux numbers and the per-light overrides all still exist — one tap further in. Nothing is typed that can be captured instead.

Controller flow Remote→ Lights→ Buttons→ Review→ API key, only if it is missing Schedule is the same with Lights → Windows → Review. Circadian, curve and daylight keep their two screens and never see a key.

Worth knowing
Give any one lamp to one Lightkeeper device. Both of these lamps are free, so nothing will fight over them.
One thing left
A schedule runs on Homey's own Flows, and Homey will not let an app write those on its own. One key from you, once, for every controller and schedule you add.
Already have one? It is reused, and you will not see this screen again.
What changed
→ Same move as 1a — key last, one shared review — but the readback is the timeline again, so the object the user just drew is the object they confirm.
→ It is also the natural home for the “one lamp, one device” warning, which the app can check at this point and no current screen mentions.
→ Reachable from repair too, which is where a user with a dead key currently lands with no idea what the device was for.
Where I would mix them

1b's day strip and timeline are worth the build — they replace explanation with a picture, and the curve driver already needs a strip like it. 1a wins on the daylight screen and on the remote: capturing a lux value beats dragging on a log axis you cannot judge, and press-to-learn is the only version where the user never reads an event name. The review screen and the key-last change are the same in both, and are the cheapest of everything here.
